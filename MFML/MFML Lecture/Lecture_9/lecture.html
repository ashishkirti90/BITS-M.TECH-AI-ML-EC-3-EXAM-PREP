01 · The problem

Fitting a Line = Optimization

The lecture opens with a tiny table: \(x = 1,2,3,4\) and \(y = 3.1,\,4.9,\,7.3,\,9.1\). The easiest model of \(y\) is a straight line, \(y = ax + b\). Which \(a\) and \(b\) are best? To answer that, we first need a number that says how bad any guess is.

Analogy first. Think of tuning an old radio with two dials to get the clearest signal. Each dial position produces some static. You listen, nudge a dial, and listen again. Fitting a model is exactly this: two dials (\(a\) and \(b\)), one static level (the loss).

Step 1 — the miss, squared

Write \(\hat y = ax + b\) for the model's prediction (the hat means "a guess"). For point \(i\), the miss is the gap \(y_i - \hat y_i\). Gaps can be positive or negative, and we do not want them cancelling. So we square each gap and add them up:

$$ L(a,b) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{4}\big(y_i - \hat y_i\big)^2 \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{4}\big(y_i - (a x_i + b)\big)^2 $$

Here \(\sum\) (the Greek capital sigma) just means "add up over all four points". Small \(L\) means the line hugs the data. Finding the \((a,b)\) with the smallest \(L\) is an optimization problem — the whole lecture in one sentence.

Worked example, real numbers. Score the guess \((a,b) = (1,1)\), so \(\hat y = x+1\) gives predictions \(2, 3, 4, 5\).
Gaps: \(1.1,\; 1.9,\; 3.3,\; 4.1\). Squares: \(1.21 + 3.61 + 10.89 + 16.81 = \textbf{32.52}\).
Now score \((2,1)\): predictions \(3,5,7,9\); gaps \(0.1, -0.1, 0.3, 0.1\); loss \(0.01+0.01+0.09+0.01 = \textbf{0.12}\).
The second line is about 271× better. The exact best pair (set both slopes of \(L\) to zero and solve) is \(a = \textbf{2.04},\; b = \textbf{1.00}\), with \(L = 0.072\).

⚡ Drive the two dials, watch the loss

Drag \(a\) and \(b\). The amber gaps stretch and shrink, and the console recomputes the full loss, term by term. What to look for: no pair of dials beats (2.04, 1.00) — press the button and try to beat it by hand.

Where this shows up in ML

This is machine learning in miniature. The line is the model, \((a,b)\) are its parameters, and \(L\) is the training loss. Linear regression, logistic regression, and GPT-class networks all repeat this recipe. Only the model and the loss get bigger.

Pitfall. \(L\) is a function of the parameters \((a,b)\), not of the data \(x\). The data are frozen into the landscape. And we square the gaps — raw gaps cancel in pairs, so a terrible line could score a perfect 0.

Bridge → So "best line" means "lowest point of \(L\)". Time to learn how to read a landscape: slopes, flat spots, valleys.

02 · The problem

Valleys & Flat Spots

We seek the minimum of a differentiable function. The gradient tells us the direction of maximum increase, so we walk the other way — and we stop where the gradient dies. But a flat spot can be a valley, a hilltop, or a pass. How do we tell?

Analogy first. You are on a hilly golf course in thick fog. The tilt under your shoes is the gradient: a direction (steepest uphill) and a strength (how steep). Walk against the tilt to descend. When the ground feels level, you have arrived — somewhere. The fog is the point: you never see the whole landscape, only the slope underfoot.

Step 1 — stationary points

For one variable, the gradient is the derivative \(\frac{dl}{dx}\): the slope. A point with slope zero is a stationary point. The slide's example is the quartic:

$$ l(x) = x^4 + 7x^3 + 5x^2 - 17x + 3, \qquad \frac{dl}{dx} = 4x^3 + 21x^2 + 10x - 17 $$

Setting the slope to zero is a cubic equation. So there are up to three such points — here, exactly three.

Step 2 — the second-derivative test

The second derivative \(\frac{d^2l}{dx^2} = 12x^2 + 42x + 10\) is the slope of the slope: the curvature. Positive means the ground bends up like a cup (a local minimum). Negative means it bends down like a dome (a local maximum).

Worked example, real numbers. The three roots of \(4x^3+21x^2+10x-17=0\) sit at \(x \approx -4.480,\; -1.432,\; 0.662\) (bracket each sign change, then bisect).
Curvature at \(-4.480\): \(12(20.07) - 188.2 + 10 = \textbf{+62.7}\) → local min, \(l = -47.08\).
Curvature at \(-1.432\): \(12(2.05) - 60.1 + 10 = \textbf{-25.5}\) → local max, \(l = 21.25\).
Curvature at \(0.662\): \(12(0.44) + 27.8 + 10 = \textbf{+43.1}\) → local min, \(l = -3.84\).
Two valleys, different depths: \(-47.08\) is the global minimum; \(-3.84\) is only local.

⚡ Walk the quartic — feel slope and curvature

Drag the point along the curve. The violet tangent is the slope; the console runs the second-derivative test live. Then press roll downhill from each start. What to look for: the two starts land in different valleys — where you begin decides what you find.

Where this shows up in ML

Deep-net losses look like this. Millions of dimensions, many valleys, different depths. Training finds a valley, not the valley. In many dimensions the curvature becomes a matrix (the Hessian), and flat spots can also be saddles — up one way, down another.

Pitfall. "Gradient = 0" does not mean "minimum". Ask the curvature before celebrating. And a local minimum need not be the global one — you just proved it on the quartic.
Check yourself: the loss of Chapter 01 had one valley. The quartic has two. What single number told the two kinds of flat spot apart?
The second derivative (curvature). Positive at a flat spot → cup → local minimum; negative → dome → local maximum. On the quartic: +62.7, −25.5, +43.1.

Bridge → Solving "slope = 0" by algebra worked for a quartic. A million-knob model needs a method that only ever uses the local slope: gradient descent.

03 · The algorithm

The Update Rule

Real models have millions of knobs and no closed-form answer. So we stop solving and start walking: from a start point, repeatedly step against the gradient. That loop is gradient descent, a first-order method — it uses slopes only, never curvatures.

The rule, symbol by symbol

$$ \boldsymbol{x}_{i+1} \;=\; \boldsymbol{x}_{i} \;-\; \gamma_i\,\big((\nabla f)(\boldsymbol{x}_i)\big)^{\!\top} $$

\(\boldsymbol{x}_i\) is the position after \(i\) steps. \(\nabla f\) (say "grad f") is the gradient: all partial slopes in one vector. \(\gamma_i\) (gamma) is the step size — ML calls it the learning rate. The \(\top\) transposes the lecture's row-vector gradient into a column. The minus sign is the whole trick: the gradient points uphill, so subtracting it walks downhill. For a small enough \(\gamma\), each step is safe: \(f(\boldsymbol{x}_0) \ge f(\boldsymbol{x}_1) \ge f(\boldsymbol{x}_2) \ge \dots\), a falling staircase into a local minimum.

The slide's example, live

The slides run this on \(f = \tfrac12 \boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\! A \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\) with \(A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&20\end{bmatrix}\), \(\boldsymbol{b} = \begin{bmatrix}-5\\-3\end{bmatrix}\) (code: grad_descent1.m). Multiplied out: \(f = x_1^2 + x_1x_2 + 10x_2^2 - 5x_1 - 3x_2\), with gradient \(\nabla f = (2x_1{+}x_2{-}5,\; x_1{+}20x_2{-}3)\).

Worked example, real numbers. The floor is where \(A\boldsymbol{x} = -\boldsymbol{b}\): substitute \(x_2 = 5-2x_1\) into \(x_1 + 20x_2 = 3\) to get \(-39x_1 = -97\), so \(x_1 = \textbf{2.4872},\; x_2 = \textbf{0.0256}\), where \(f = -6.2564\).
Now walk there from \(\boldsymbol{x}_0 = (-3,-1)\) with \(\gamma = 0.085\). Gradient: \((2(-3)-1-5,\; -3-20-3) = (-12, -26)\).
Step: \(\boldsymbol{x}_1 = (-3,-1) - 0.085\,(-12,-26) = (\textbf{-1.98},\, \textbf{1.21})\); loss falls \(40 \to 22.44\).
Again: gradient \((-7.75, 19.22)\), so \(\boldsymbol{x}_2 = (\textbf{-1.3213},\, \textbf{-0.4237})\); loss \(\to 11.98\). The staircase is falling.

⚡ Walk the narrow bowl

Press step and watch the exact arithmetic in the console. What to look for: the bowl is 10× steeper in \(x_2\) (the 20 in \(A\)). So the path zig-zags across the valley while crawling along it. Then push \(\gamma\) past 0.0997 — the largest curvature snaps the walk apart.

Where this shows up in ML

This loop is the training loop. Backpropagation computes \(\nabla f\) for every weight at once; the optimiser applies exactly this update. First-order only is why it scales: slopes for a billion weights are affordable, curvatures are not.

Pitfall. The guarantee is doubly local: the step must be small enough. And the destination is only a local minimum — whichever valley your start drains into.

Bridge → Two knobs still means a 2-D map. Real losses are surfaces. Let's look at one properly — in 3-D.

04 · The algorithm

The Loss Landscape in 3-D

With two weights the loss is a surface: height = loss. Gradient descent is a ball rolling on it. And the lecture's warning — "converges to some local minimum" — becomes visible. A bumpy landscape has more than one resting place.

Analogy first. A convex bowl is a kitchen sink: wherever you release the ball, it finds the one drain. A non-convex landscape is an egg carton: two dips, and the ridge between them decides which dip catches your ball.

⚡ Roll the ball — then make the landscape bumpy

Drag the surface to rotate it (plain canvas, no 3-D library). In convex bowl mode both presets reach the same green drain. Switch to two valleys: presets A and B start almost side by side, yet land in different minima. That is why initialisation matters.

Where this shows up in ML

Real losses are bumpy. Deep nets are wildly non-convex, yet SGD-trained nets work. Part of the story: in very high dimension most bad "flat spots" are saddles, not deep traps. The two-valley toy is the smallest honest picture of that world.

Pitfall. Convexity is a gift, not a given. Linear regression's bowl (Chapter 01) has one drain; a neural net's landscape does not. Never assume the valley you found is the deepest one.

Bridge → Every run so far quietly depended on one number: \(\gamma\). Time to stress-test it.

05 · The algorithm

Choosing the Step Size

The slide's dilemma. Too small a step size, and gradient descent is painfully slow. Too large, and it overshoots, fails to converge, or diverges outright. The gradient promises nothing beyond your toes — \(\gamma\) is how far you leap on that promise.

Analogy first. Walking downstairs in the dark: shuffle one toe-length at a time and take all night. Or jump three steps and crack your shin on the far wall. The stairs did not change. Your stride did.

The three fates, computed

On \(f(x) = x^2\) the update collapses to one multiplication: \(x \leftarrow x - \gamma(2x) = (1-2\gamma)x\). The factor \((1-2\gamma)\) decides everything:

γfactor 1−2γiterates from 4fate
0.050.94 → 3.6 → 3.24 → 2.916…crawls
0.450.14 → 0.4 → 0.04 → 0.004glides
1.05−1.14 → −4.4 → 4.84 → −5.32…explodes

Shrinking needs \(|1-2\gamma| < 1\), which solves to \(0 < \gamma < 1\). At exactly \(\gamma = 1\) the ball bounces between \(\pm 4\) forever.

The slide's fix: undo and halve

Make \(\gamma\) adaptive. Try a step. Loss went up? You were too aggressive: undo the step and halve \(\gamma\). Loss went down? Keep it, and let the next step be a bit larger. The recorded loss then never rises — that is the slide's "guarantees monotonic convergence".

⚡ Find the divergence edge — then let it heal itself

Slide \(\gamma\) and step. What to look for: below 1 the ball settles; above 1 each bounce grows. Then set \(\gamma = 1.2\), switch on undo-and-halve, and watch the console reject the bad step, halve \(\gamma\), and glide home.

Where this shows up in ML

This is deep learning's most-tuned knob. Divergence even has a famous signature: the loss becomes NaN. Undo-and-halve lives on inside "reduce LR on plateau" schedulers and gradient-clipping safety nets.

Pitfall. "Monotonic" is not "fast". Halve too eagerly and \(\gamma\) shrinks to nothing — the run stalls politely. And a \(\gamma\) that is safe on one slope can be reckless on a steeper one.
Check yourself: on \(f(x)=x^2\), why does \(\gamma = 0.5\) reach the minimum in exactly one step?
The update multiplies \(x\) by \((1-2\gamma)\). At \(\gamma=0.5\) that factor is 0, so any start maps straight to \(x=0\) — the minimum — in one hop.

Bridge → Guess-and-check is one answer. For a quadratic bowl there is a better one: calculus can hand us the perfect step.

06 · Smarter steps

The Exact Best Step

Freeze the direction, and the loss along it becomes a one-variable function of \(\gamma\). One variable means calculus can minimise it exactly. The slide does this for \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 3y^2\) from \((2,2)\) — code: grad_descent2.m.

Build \(h(\gamma)\), then differentiate

The gradient is \(\nabla f = [2x,\, 6y]\), so one step moves \(x \to (1-2\gamma)x\) and \(y \to (1-6\gamma)y\). Substitute into \(f\):

$$ h(\gamma) = (1-2\gamma)^2 x^2 + 3\,(1-6\gamma)^2 y^2, \qquad h'(\gamma) = -4(1-2\gamma)x^2 - 36(1-6\gamma)y^2 $$

Set \(h'(\gamma) = 0\), expand, and collect \(\gamma\):

$$ \gamma \;=\; \frac{x^2 + 9y^2}{2x^2 + 54y^2} $$

The perfect step, recomputed fresh at every point.

Worked example, real numbers. At \((2,2)\): \(x^2 = y^2 = 4\), so \(\gamma = \frac{4+36}{8+216} = \frac{40}{224} = \frac{5}{28} \approx \textbf{0.1786}\).
Move: \(x_1 = 0.6429 \times 2 = \textbf{1.2857}\), \(y_1 = -0.0714 \times 2 = \textbf{-0.1429}\). Loss: \(16 \to \textbf{1.7143}\) — one step erased 89%.
Next point: \(\gamma = \frac{1.6531 + 0.1837}{3.3061 + 1.1020} = \frac{5}{12} \approx \textbf{0.4167}\), landing at \((0.2143,\, 0.2143)\), loss \(\textbf{0.1837}\). The two step sizes then alternate: \(\tfrac{5}{28}, \tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{5}{28}, \dots\).

⚡ Take perfect steps — and catch the 90° secret

Each press computes \(\gamma\) from the formula and takes the best possible step. What to look for: the console checks (new gradient) · (old direction) after every move — it is always zero. Perfect steps land exactly where the new downhill is sideways, so the path staircases at right angles.

Where this shows up in ML

This "bead on a wire" trick seeds every line search. It also powers the learning-rate finders in modern libraries, which probe a range of \(\gamma\) before training. The slide's caution stands: finding the step can be expensive — Chapters 10–13 price that trade.

Pitfall. The tidy formula exists because \(f\) is quadratic. For a general loss every probe of \(h(\gamma)\) costs a full model evaluation.

Bridge → One question before trusting any of this on your own model. Is your gradient code even right?

07 · Smarter steps

Gradient Checking

Gradient descent trusts the gradient completely. If your derivative code has a bug, the walk marches confidently the wrong way. The slide's safety net: compare your formula against a purely numerical estimate that needs no calculus at all.

Analogy first. To check a car's speedometer you do not open the engine. You time yourself between two mile markers and divide distance by time. Finite differences are the mile-marker test for your gradient code.

The finite-difference formula

Let \(J(\boldsymbol{w}) = J(w_1, \dots, w_d)\). Pick one parameter \(w_i\), nudge it by a small \(\Delta\) (capital delta), and leave the rest alone:

$$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_i} \;\approx\; \frac{J(w_1,\dots,w_i + \Delta,\dots,w_d) \;-\; J(w_1,\dots,w_i,\dots,w_d)}{\Delta} $$

Top: how much \(J\) rose. Bottom: how far we pushed. Because two evaluations per parameter is slow, the slide says to sample a few parameters and spot-check those, not all \(d\).

Worked example, real numbers. Take \(J(w_1,w_2) = w_1^2 + 3w_1w_2\) at \((1,2)\). Calculus claims \(\partial J/\partial w_1 = 2w_1 + 3w_2 = \textbf{8}\).
Check: \(J(1,2) = 7\) and \(J(1.01,\,2) = 1.0201 + 6.06 = 7.0801\). Estimate: \((7.0801-7)/0.01 = \textbf{8.0100}\).
The 0.01 mismatch is the method, not a bug: the one-sided probe picks up half the curvature times \(\Delta\), here \(\tfrac12(2)(0.01) = 0.01\). For \(w_2\): claim \(3w_1 = 3\); estimate \((7.03-7)/0.01 = \textbf{3.0000}\), exact, because \(J\) is straight in \(w_2\).

⚡ Shrink Δ and watch the secant become the tangent

The amber secant joins \(w\) and \(w+\Delta\) on the slice \(J(w_1, 2)\). Drag \(\Delta\) toward zero: the secant rotates onto the violet tangent, and the error tracks \(\Delta\) exactly. Then toggle the central difference — the curvature term cancels and the error collapses.

Where this shows up in ML

This is gradient checking, the classic unit test for hand-written backpropagation. Frameworks automate it (torch.autograd.gradcheck), comparing autograd against finite differences on a few sampled coordinates — exactly the slide's advice.

Pitfall. \(\Delta\) is a compromise. Too large: curvature pollutes the estimate. Too small: floating-point subtraction destroys the numerator. Around \(10^{-5}\) works well; central differences buy an extra order of accuracy.

Bridge → Gradient verified. Back to the rate itself: what if it should not be one constant at all, but a number that shrinks over time?

08 · Smarter steps

Learning-Rate Decay

A large constant rate races close to the optimum — then oscillates around it forever. Each step leaps across the minimum to the far side. A small constant takes a very long time to arrive. The slides' answer: start large, shrink with time.

Analogy first. Parking a car: cover the lot at speed, brake near the bay, inch the last half-metre. Highway speed the whole way overshoots forever; parking speed the whole way never arrives.

The schedules

With a time-dependent rate the update is \(\boldsymbol{w}_{n+1} = \boldsymbol{w}_n - \alpha_t \nabla J\). Here \(t\) counts epochs: full cycles over the training data. So the rate stays constant within one cycle. The two most common decay functions, plus the staircase:

$$ \text{exponential: } \alpha_t = \alpha_0\, e^{-kt} \qquad \text{inverse: } \alpha_t = \frac{\alpha_0}{1+kt} \qquad \text{step: cut by a fixed factor every few epochs} $$

\(\alpha_0\) is the starting rate; \(k\) controls how fast the shrinking happens.

Worked example, real numbers. \(\alpha_0 = 0.5\), \(k = 0.5\).
Exponential: \(\alpha_1 = 0.5e^{-0.5} = 0.5 \times 0.6065 = \textbf{0.3033}\); then \(0.1839,\; 0.1116,\; 0.0677\).
Inverse: \(\alpha_1 = 0.5/1.5 = \textbf{0.3333}\); then \(0.25,\; 0.20,\; 0.1667\).
By epoch 4 the exponential rate has lost 86% of its size, the inverse only 67%: exponential brakes hard, inverse keeps a long tail.

⚡ Same start, four rate policies — who lands?

Left: the schedule \(\alpha_t\). Right: a descent on \(f(x)=x^2\) from \(x=4\) using it. What to look for: constant-large orbits the minimum forever — watch the endless zig-zag. Every decay schedule spends its boldness early and then settles. Raise \(k\) too far and the run stalls short — the other failure.

Where this shows up in ML

Every serious training run schedules its rate. Step decay trained the classic ResNets. Exponential and inverse decay are one-liners in every framework. Modern recipes add warm-up and cosine shapes. The reason is exactly the slide's: constant-large dances, constant-small crawls.

Pitfall. Decay that is too aggressive freezes the run far from the valley. And \(\alpha_0\) still matters — no schedule rescues a divergent first epoch.

Bridge → Schedules shrink the rate blindly, on a timer. The bold driver listens instead.

09 · Smarter steps

The Bold Driver

Adjust the learning rate by results, every iteration. Improving? Grow the rate about 5% per step. Worsened? Undo the step, cut the rate by 50%, and take a fresh step. Continue to convergence.

Analogy first. Drive bolder while the road rewards you. The moment you clip a kerb, back up and drive at half speed. Improvement earns boldness; a crash costs half of it.
Worked example, real numbers (\(f = x^2\), \(x_0 = 4\), \(\alpha_0 = 0.9\)).
It 1: try \(4 - 0.9(8) = -3.2\); \(f: 16 \to 10.24\), better → keep, \(\alpha = 0.945\).
It 2: try \(2.848\); \(f \to 8.111\), better → \(\alpha = 0.992\).
It 3: try \(-2.804\); \(f \to 7.862\), barely better → \(\alpha = 1.042\).
It 4: try \(3.039\); \(f \to 9.233\), worse → undo, \(\alpha = 0.521\).
It 5: fresh try \(0.117\); \(f \to \textbf{0.0138}\) — a spectacular recovery. It 6: \(f \to 0.0001\). Done.

⚡ Step through the run — watch it crash and recover

Top strip: the kept loss. Bottom strip: the rate creeping up 5% per win. What to look for: iteration 4. Three greedy raises push \(\alpha\) past this bowl's safe ceiling of 1. The crash is caught (red). The halved rate then lands almost at the minimum in one hop.

Where this shows up in ML

Bold driver has many descendants. "Reduce LR on plateau" schedulers share its reflex. So do trust-ratio methods and per-weight adapters like Adagrad and Adam: listen to progress, resize the step. One caveat from the slide: mini-batch loss readings are noisy, so judge improvement over \(m\) steps, not one.

Pitfall. On noisy losses, raw bold driver halves on unlucky batches and grows on lucky ones. Use the \(m\)-step verdict, and cap how big \(\alpha\) may grow.
Check yourself: bold driver grows the rate 5% per win but halves it on a loss. Why is the punishment so much bigger than the reward?
Divergence compounds: each too-big step lands farther out, so one bad rate can undo many good steps. Growing gently probes the ceiling; halving hard retreats below it immediately. (Compare: 15 wins of +5% roughly double the rate; one halving undoes all 15.)

Bridge → Undo-and-halve and bold driver are clever guessing. The principled tool — searching the step directly — is line search.

10 · Line search

Line Search & the 90° Rule

Pick the direction first; then search that line for the truly best step. Expensive — rarely used in vanilla gradient descent — but vital in some specialised methods, and full of geometry.

Setup and names

Let \(J(\boldsymbol{w})\) be the objective and \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) the parameters at step \(t\). The descent direction is \(\boldsymbol{g}_t\); in steepest descent it is \(-\nabla J(\boldsymbol{w}_t)\). The update and the search:

$$ \boldsymbol{w}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{w}_t + \alpha_t\,\boldsymbol{g}_t, \qquad \alpha_t = \arg\min_{\alpha}\, J(\boldsymbol{w}_t + \alpha\,\boldsymbol{g}_t) $$

The perpendicular property, proved gently

Claim: at the perfect \(\alpha_t\), the new gradient \(\nabla J(\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1})\) is perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{g}_t\). Why: suppose their dot product were not zero. A tiny extra move \(\pm\delta\,\boldsymbol{g}_t\) along the same line changes \(J\) by about (Taylor's first-order rule):

$$ J(\boldsymbol{w}_t + \alpha_t\boldsymbol{g}_t \pm \delta\,\boldsymbol{g}_t) \;\approx\; J(\boldsymbol{w}_t + \alpha_t\boldsymbol{g}_t) \;\pm\; \delta\, \boldsymbol{g}_t^{\top} \nabla J(\boldsymbol{w}_t + \alpha_t\boldsymbol{g}_t) $$

Positive dot product? Choose the minus sign. Negative? The plus. Either way \(J\) drops — contradicting "\(\alpha_t\) was the minimum". So the dot product must be zero. You verified this numerically in Chapter 06: \((-4)(2.5714) + (-12)(-0.8571) = 0\), exactly.

Bracketing the dip

How to actually minimise over \(\alpha\)? In typical settings \(J\) along the line is unimodal in \(\alpha\). It falls, bottoms out once, rises — no second dip. That holds especially under quadratic or convex approximations. So first trap the dip in a bracket \([0, \alpha_{\max}]\): evaluate at geometrically growing \(\alpha\) until the value rises. Then narrow the bracket. Binary search and golden-section search are exact and need unimodality. The Armijo rule is inexact, needs no unimodality, and applies more broadly.

⚡ Scrub the 1-D curve, then sweep to bracket it

This is \(h(\alpha)\) for the first step from \((2,2)\) on \(x^2+3y^2\). Hover to scrub: read the loss any step size would give. Then press sweep: probes at 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5 fall, fall, fall, rise. So the dip is trapped in [0, 0.5] before we ever "solve" anything.

Where this shows up in ML

This is the classical base case. Its right-angle staircase motivates conjugate-gradient methods, which bend the next direction to stop the zig-zag. Newton-type methods lean on line searches too.

Pitfall. The 90° rule holds only at the exact line minimum — stop early and it fails. And without unimodality, bracket methods can zoom confidently onto the wrong dip.

Bridge → Bracket in hand, two exact narrowing tools. First: read one slope and throw away half.

11 · Line search

Binary Search

Hold a bracket \([a,b] = [0, \alpha_{\max}]\) containing the single dip. Ask one question at the midpoint: rising or falling here? Rising → the dip is behind (left). Falling → ahead (right). Either way, half the bracket dies.

Analogy first. Finding a word in a paper dictionary: open to the middle, check one thing — past it or before it? — and ignore half the book. Here "alphabetical order" becomes "which way the curve tilts".

Reading the tilt without a formula

The slide's trick: evaluate at two squeezed-together points. Use the midpoint \(\frac{a+b}{2}\) and its neighbour \(\frac{a+b+\epsilon}{2}\), with \(\epsilon\) tiny (about \(10^{-6}\)). Second value larger → increasing → keep \(\big[a, \frac{a+b+\epsilon}{2}\big]\). Otherwise keep \(\big[\frac{a+b}{2}, b\big]\). Repeat.

Worked example, real numbers (on \(h(\gamma)\) from Chapter 06, bracket [0, 0.5], true dip at 0.1786).
Round 1: mid 0.25, \(h = 4.0\), rising → [0, 0.25].
Round 2: mid 0.125, \(h = 3.0\), falling → [0.125, 0.25].
Round 3: mid 0.1875, \(h = 1.75\), rising → [0.125, 0.1875].
Round 4: mid 0.15625, \(h = 1.9375\), falling → [0.15625, 0.1875].
Width: 0.5 → 0.03125, a factor of \(2^4 = 16\), and the dip never escaped. Cost: two evaluations per round.

⚡ Probe the midpoint, kill half the bracket

Press probe repeatedly. The dropped half turns red; the surviving bracket tightens around the amber dip. What to look for: the width halves every round — precision doubles at linear cost.

Where this shows up in ML

Interval halving on a 1-D response is everywhere. Learning-rate range probes reuse the loop. So do threshold tuning and bisection on regularisation strength. Same loop, different curve.

Pitfall. \(\epsilon\) must beat floating-point noise or the rising/falling verdict becomes a coin flip. And unimodality is load-bearing: with two dips, a discarded half can contain the better one.

Bridge → Binary search reads slopes. The next tool never does — it only compares values.

12 · Line search

Golden-Section Search

Keep two interior sample points \(m_1 < m_2\) inside \([a,b]\) and evaluate all four positions. Then drop the stretch that provably cannot hold the minimum. No derivatives, no probes — just comparisons.

Analogy first. Four scouts report their altitude. On a one-dip valley, the lowest scout cannot be separated from the dip by a higher scout. Whatever lies beyond a higher neighbour is doomed territory — cut it off the map.

The dropping rules (from the slide)

  • smallest value at \(a\): drop \((m_1, b]\).
  • smallest value at \(b\): drop \([a, m_2)\).
  • smallest value at \(m_1\): drop \((m_2, b]\).
  • smallest value at \(m_2\): drop \([a, m_1)\).

Sometimes both end pieces go at once. Then re-seed. Survivor holds no evaluated point? Pick a random \(p\) inside, and a \(q\) in the larger piece \(p\) creates. Holds exactly one point \(p\)? Add \(q\) in the larger of \([a,p]\) and \([p,b]\). Four points again; repeat to the accuracy you need.

Worked example, real numbers (same \(h\), bracket [0, 0.5], thirds as mid-samples).
Values: \(h(0)=16\), \(h(1/6)=\textbf{1.778}\) (the \(12(1-6\gamma)^2\) term dies at \(\gamma = 1/6\)), \(h(1/3)=12.444\), \(h(0.5)=48\).
Smallest at \(m_1\) → drop \((m_2, b]\) → survivor [0, 1/3] holding \(p = 1/6\).
Re-seed \(q = 0.25\) (\(h = 4\)). New four: 0, 1/6, 0.25, 1/3. Smallest at 1/6 → drop \((0.25, 1/3]\) → [0, 0.25]. The dip (0.1786) survived both cuts, as the rules guarantee.

⚡ Apply the drop rule, round by round

Each press evaluates the four points and names the smallest. It shades the doomed piece red, then re-seeds. What to look for: the console cites which rule fired each round — value comparisons alone corner the minimum.

Where this shows up in ML

Why "golden"? Space the interior points a fraction \((\sqrt{5}-1)/2 \approx 0.618\) of the width from each end. Then every cut leaves one old point exactly where the next round needs it. Cost: one new evaluation per round, versus binary search's two. Golden-section and its parabolic cousin, Brent's method, power minimize_scalar in scientific Python.

Pitfall. The drop rules are proofs only under unimodality. Feed in a two-dip curve and the method politely discards the better dip.

Bridge → We now own precise, pricey tools. The engineering question: when do they pay?

13 · Line search

When Is It Worth It?

Line search provably converges to a local optimum — but each step costs many evaluations. That is why vanilla gradient descent rarely uses it. Newton's method has no choice: it needs exact line search. The everyday compromise is Armijo's rule. Try a step. If the drop is not at least a set fraction of what the slope promised, shrink and retry.

Analogy first. Crossing a city, you can study the map at every corner: slow corners, few wrong turns. Or stride on instinct: instant corners, several wrong turns. The fastest trip glances at the map only when the road looks confusing.
The bill that decides. Total work = (steps taken) × (evaluations per step). A stylised bill. Fixed step: 400 × 1 = 400. Armijo: 150 × 2 = 300. Exact search: 40 × 12 = 480. The middle ground wins here — but change the ratios and the winner flips. That is the slide's point: fewer steps "might more than compensate" the per-step cost.

⚡ Price the three policies yourself

Drag how many steps exact search saves, and what each of its steps costs. What to look for: the winning bar flips as you drag — neither precision nor cheapness wins by default; only the product does.

Where this shows up in ML

Deep-learning loops choose schedules. Per-step search across billions of weights and noisy mini-batches is unaffordable. Classical solvers (BFGS family, conjugate gradient) keep an inexact line search; Newton-style methods keep the exact kind.

Pitfall. Comparing optimisers by iteration count alone is a classic trap. Half the steps at ten times the per-step work is five times slower. Price the product.
Check yourself: your exact line search now needs only 4 evaluations per step and 40 steps. Fixed-step still needs 400 cheap steps. Who wins?
Exact search: 40 × 4 = 160 evaluations against 400. The product decides — and cheap, accurate per-step searches are exactly when line search earns its price.

Bridge → One cost we have ignored all along: every gradient so far read the whole dataset. Time to fix that.

14 · Scaling up

Stochastic Gradient Descent

A training loss is a sum over data points: \(L(\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} L_n(\theta)\), with \(\theta\) the parameters and \(N\) the number of points. Batch gradient descent differentiates the whole sum — every step reads all \(N\) points. With \(N\) in the millions, one step is a full pass over the data.

Analogy first. To season a huge pot of soup you do not drink the pot. Stir, taste a spoonful, adjust, repeat. The spoon misjudges a little every time — but it is nearly free, and repeated rounds land the seasoning. Stir first: the spoon must be a random sample.

Sample the data, keep the rule

Let \(S\) be a random subset of indices. Build the sample-only objective and swap its gradient into the update:

$$ J(S) = \sum_{i \in S} \big( \boldsymbol{w}^{\top} \boldsymbol{X}_i - y_i \big)^2, \qquad \boldsymbol{w} \;\leftarrow\; \boldsymbol{w} - \alpha \left[ \frac{\partial J(S)}{\partial w_1},\, \dots,\, \frac{\partial J(S)}{\partial w_d} \right]^{\top} $$

A moderate \(S\) is mini-batch gradient descent; \(|S| = 1\) is SGD proper. Why it works: the sampled gradient is an unbiased estimate, right on average over the draw. So errors cancel across many steps. With a suitably decreasing rate (and mild assumptions), SGD converges almost surely (probability one) to a local minimum. And batch size sets the noise: larger mini-batches, lower variance.

Worked example, real numbers (Chapter 01's data, \(\boldsymbol{X}_i = (x_i, 1)\), at \(\boldsymbol{w} = (0,0)\)).
Per-point gradients \(2 (\boldsymbol{w}^{\top} \boldsymbol{X}_i - y_i) \boldsymbol{X}_i\): \((-6.2,-6.2)\), \((-19.6,-9.8)\), \((-43.8,-14.6)\), \((-72.8,-18.2)\).
Full gradient (the sum): \((\textbf{-142.4},\, \textbf{-48.8})\).
Unbiasedness, live: draw one point uniformly, scale by \(N = 4\); the four possible estimates average to exactly \((-142.4, -48.8)\).
Direction error vs the truth: single points miss by 26.1°, 7.7°, 0.5°, 4.9° (mean 9.8°); two-point batches mean 4.3°; full batch 0°. Bigger spoonfuls, straighter aim.

⚡ Train the Chapter-01 line by spoonfuls

Choose the batch size and run. What to look for: batch 1 wanders, batch 4 glides — same landscape, different noise. With a constant rate the batch-1 walk jitters around the star forever; switch on decaying rate and the jitter spends itself down. Reshuffle to see a different random story.

Where this shows up in ML

It trains modern AI. Language models, vision models, recommenders — all of them. Epoch, mini-batch, batch size, schedule: the daily dialect of deep learning. Adam, the default optimiser, is SGD wearing per-weight step sizes.

Pitfall. SGD's noise never dies on its own: constant rate → eternal jitter around the minimum. The convergence promise needs a decreasing rate (Chapter 08). And judge progress over many steps, not one (Chapter 09's \(m\)-step rule).
Check yourself: one random point gives a gradient that is wrong at almost every step. Why does the walk still reach the valley?
The estimate is unbiased — right on average — so the errors cancel across steps. A decaying rate shrinks the leftover jitter. Roughly right, many times, cheaply, beats exactly right once.

15 · Wrap up

Key Takeaways

One move: step against the gradient. Everything else in the lecture refines how far to step and how much data to consult.

The problem

Fitting = minimising a loss over parameters. Flat spots are candidates; curvature sorts minima from maxima; valleys come in local and global.

The rule

\(\boldsymbol{x}_{i+1} = \boldsymbol{x}_i - \gamma_i (\nabla f)^{\top}\). Small enough steps give a falling staircase into a local minimum. First-order = scalable.

The step size

Crawl, glide, or explode. Fixes: undo-and-halve, decay schedules (\(\alpha_0 e^{-kt}\), \(\alpha_0/(1{+}kt)\), step), bold driver (+5% / undo & halve).

Line search

Freeze the direction; minimise the 1-D curve \(h(\alpha)\). Perfect steps land at 90°. Bracket the dip, then binary or golden-section narrows it.

The economics

Total work = steps × work per step. Exact search: few pricey steps. Armijo: the everyday compromise. Newton demands the exact kind.

SGD

Sample the data; the gradient stays right on average. Noise shrinks with batch size and with a decaying rate. This trains modern AI.

The thread. Chapter 01's four little points became a loss. The loss became a landscape; the landscape got walked. The walk got a smarter stride. And the stride finally learned to read only a spoonful of data at a time. That spoonful-walk is running, right now, inside every large model you have heard of.
