Demystifying PCA: The Math & The Intuition

1. The Goal: Maximizing Variance

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a powerful method for dimensionality reduction. Imagine you have data points in 2D (like the visualization below), but you want to represent them using a single number (1D). Which line should you project them onto?

The Intuition of "Shadows": Think of the projection as casting a shadow of the data points onto a wall (a line). If the wall is perpendicular to the data's spread, the shadow will be a small, dense clump. We lose the "shape" of the data. However, if the wall aligns with the widest spread of the data, the shadow will be wide and retain the relative distances between points.

Why Variance? High variance = High Information. If a variable doesn't change (variance = 0), it tells us nothing. We want to find the axis where the "shadow" (projection) has the maximum possible variance.

2. The Data Matrix & Centering

Let our dataset be a matrix \( X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} \), where \( n \) is the number of samples (rows) and \( d \) is the number of features (columns). $$ X = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1,1} & \dots & x_{1,d} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{n,1} & \dots & x_{n,d} \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 1: Centering. We shift the origin of our coordinate system to the center of mass of the data. We calculate the mean vector \( \bar{x} \) and subtract it from every row. This ensures that the new mean is exactly 0. $$ x_{new} = x_{old} - \bar{x} $$ This simplification is crucial because it allows us to calculate variance simply as the sum of squared values, without needing to subtract the mean again later.

3. Intuition: What does Covariance tell us?

Before doing any projection, let's look at the relationship between our features. The Covariance Matrix \( C \) summarizes how our variables move together. For 2D data (x, y), it looks like this: $$ C = \begin{bmatrix} \text{Var}(x) & \text{Cov}(x,y) \\ \text{Cov}(y,x) & \text{Var}(y) \end{bmatrix} $$

  • Diagonal Terms (\(\text{Var}(x), \text{Var}(y)\)): Describe the spread along each original axis.
  • Off-Diagonal Terms (\(\text{Cov}(x,y)\)): Describe the correlation.
    • Positive: When X increases, Y tends to increase (Data looks like /).
    • Negative: When X increases, Y tends to decrease (Data looks like \).
    • Zero: No linear relationship (Data looks like a round cloud).

The PCA Insight: If covariance is non-zero, there is "redundancy". PCA rotates our axes to align with the data's natural shape, making the new off-diagonal terms zero. This means the new features (Principal Components) are uncorrelated.

4. Projecting the Data

We seek a unit vector \( w \) (\( ||w|| = 1 \)) representing our new axis. To project a data vector \( x_i \) onto \( w \), we use the Dot Product. $$ z_i = x_i \cdot w = x_i^T w $$

Geometrically, since \( ||w||=1 \), this value \( z_i \) represents the length of the vector \( x_i \) along the direction of \( w \).

In matrix form, the vector of all projections \( z \in \mathbb{R}^n \) is: $$ z = Xw $$

5. The Variance of the Projection

We want to maximize the variance of \( z \). Since we centered \( X \), the mean of \( z \) is also 0. $$ \text{Var}(z) = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (z_i - 0)^2 $$

Let's expand this using matrix algebra. The sum of squares is the dot product of the vector \( z \) with itself: $$ \sum z_i^2 = z^T z $$ Substituting \( z = Xw \): $$ z^T z = (Xw)^T (Xw) = w^T X^T X w $$

Therefore, the variance is: $$ \text{Var}(z) = \frac{1}{n-1} w^T X^T X w = w^T \left( \frac{X^T X}{n-1} \right) w $$

The term in the middle is the Sample Covariance Matrix, \( C \). $$ \text{Objective: Maximize } w^T C w \quad \text{ subject to } w^T w = 1 $$

6. The Dual Perspective: Minimizing Error

There is another way to look at PCA. Instead of maximizing variance along the projection, we can think about minimizing the reconstruction error.

Consider a single data point vector \( x_i \). We project it onto \( w \) to get the approximation \( \hat{x}_i = (x_i^T w)w \). The error vector is the difference: \( e_i = x_i - \hat{x}_i \).

Pythagorean Theorem in High Dimensions: Since the error vector is orthogonal to the projection, we have: $$ ||x_i||^2 = ||\hat{x}_i||^2 + ||e_i||^2 $$

Summing over all points: $$ \sum ||x_i||^2 = \sum ||\hat{x}_i||^2 + \sum ||e_i||^2 $$

The Insight: The term \( \sum ||x_i||^2 \) is constant (it's just the total energy of our dataset). Therefore, Maximizing the projection length (\( \sum ||\hat{x}_i||^2 \), which is variance) is mathematically equivalent to Minimizing the error (\( \sum ||e_i||^2 \)).

7. The Proof: Lagrange Multipliers

To solve this constrained optimization problem, we define the Lagrangian \( \mathcal{L} \). We introduce a Lagrange multiplier \( \lambda \) to enforce the constraint \( w^T w = 1 \). $$ \mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^T C w - \lambda (w^T w - 1) $$

Step-by-Step Derivative:
1. Derivative of \( w^T C w \) w.r.t \( w \): Since \( C \) is symmetric, this is \( 2Cw \).
2. Derivative of \( \lambda w^T w \) w.r.t \( w \): This is \( 2\lambda w \).
3. Set the total derivative to zero: $$ \nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 2Cw - 2\lambda w = 0 $$

Dividing by 2, we get the famous Eigenvalue Equation: $$ Cw = \lambda w $$

Interpretation: The optimal direction \( w \) must be an Eigenvector of the Covariance Matrix. But which one? Let's check the variance value at this solution: $$ \text{Variance} = w^T C w = w^T (\lambda w) = \lambda (w^T w) = \lambda (1) = \lambda $$

The variance is exactly equal to the Eigenvalue \( \lambda \). To maximize variance, we must choose the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue.

8. Connection to SVD

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) decomposes the data matrix \( X \) directly: $$ X = U \Sigma V^T $$ Where \( \Sigma \) is a diagonal matrix of singular values \( \sigma_i \).

Let's derive the covariance matrix \( C \) using SVD: $$ C \propto X^T X = (U \Sigma V^T)^T (U \Sigma V^T) = V \Sigma^T U^T U \Sigma V^T $$

Since \( U \) is orthogonal, \( U^T U = I \). Also \( \Sigma \) is diagonal, so \( \Sigma^T = \Sigma \). $$ C \propto V \Sigma^2 V^T $$

This matches the Eigendecomposition form \( V \Lambda V^T \). Thus:

  • The Right Singular Vectors (\( V \)) of \( X \) are the Principal Components.
  • The Singular Values (\( \sigma \)) are related to variances (\( \lambda \)) by \( \lambda_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{n-1} \).

9. Why is this Important?

Understanding PCA provides intuition for many advanced topics in Machine Learning:

  • Feature Engineering: It automatically discovers the "most important" features, combining correlated variables into a single signal.
  • Data Compression: As we will see below, we can reconstruct complex data (like images) using only a fraction of the original information, saving huge amounts of storage.
  • Noise Reduction: The lower principal components often contain "noise" or random variations. Dropping them can actually improve model performance by preventing overfitting.
