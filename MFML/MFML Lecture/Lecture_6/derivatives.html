The Geometry of the Power Rule

Forget the algebra. The whole proof is one instruction you can say out loud — and then just count the new pieces.

The proof, in one sentence

“Grow the side a little… and count the new strips.”

1

A square, growing by a sliver

A square of side $x$ has area $x^2$. Push the side out by a tiny $h$. Drag the slider and watch what appears — and what becomes too small to matter.

slide the side out  ·  h 0.30
original area  x²1.00
two strips  2·x·h0.60
corner crumb  h²0.09
growth per unit of h  =  2x + h
2.30→ 2x = 2.00

The crumb is real but tiny — a sliver times a sliver.

So the area grows by two strips, each of size $x\,h$ — a total of $\approx 2x\,h$. The corner crumb $h^2$ is a sliver-times-a-sliver, far too small to count. Growth per unit of $h$ is $2x$. That is the derivative. The 2 is literally how many strips you added.

2

Up one dimension: the cube

A cube of side $x$ has volume $x^3$. Grow the side by $h$ and you paste a thin slab onto three faces. Drag to rotate; slide to grow.

grow the side  ·  h 0.30
original volume  x³1.00
three slabs  3·x²·h0.90
edges + corner0.11
growth per unit of h  =  3x² + 3xh + h²
3.39→ 3x² = 3.00

Three faces grow → rate $= 3x^2$. The edge bars and the corner cube are higher-order slivers — they vanish.

3

The pattern shows itself

Once you’ve seen it twice, the exponent stops being a rule to memorise. It is just a headcount: how many faces are there to grow?

ShapeSizeFaces to growRate of growth
Segment1–D $x$ 1  new end $1 = 1\cdot x^{0}$
Square2–D $x^2$ 2  strips $2x$
Cube3–D $x^3$ 3  slabs $3x^2$
Tesseract4–D $x^4$ 4  cells $4x^3$
n–cubeany dimension $x^{n}$ n  faces $n\,x^{\,n-1}$

The exponent comes down because it counts the faces.
The power drops by one because each face is one dimension thinner.
