01

Rolle's Theorem

Start a smooth round trip and end at the same height you began. Whatever you did in between — climb, dive, wiggle — at some moment you were moving neither up nor down: a perfectly flat instant.

The intuition

Think of a drone flight that takes off and lands on the same shelf. If it ever goes above the shelf, it must come back down — so somewhere it stops rising and starts falling. At that turning instant, its vertical speed is exactly zero. The tangent is horizontal.

Smoothness is what makes this airtight: no teleporting (continuity), no sharp corners (differentiability). A V-shaped path flips from falling to rising without ever being flat — that's why corners break the theorem.

If f is continuous on [a, b], differentiable on (a, b), and f(a) = f(b), then ∃ c ∈ (a, b) : f′(c) = 0

02

Mean Value Theorem

Draw the straight line between the two endpoints — the secant, the "average direction" of the whole journey. Somewhere along the way, your instantaneous direction matched it exactly.

The intuition

Drive 120 km in 2 hours and your average speed is 60 km/h. You may have crawled and you may have raced — but at some instant your speedometer read exactly 60. You can't average 60 while always being above it or always below it.

Geometrically: slide a tangent line along the curve. Its slope starts somewhere, ends somewhere, and varies smoothly — so on the way it must sweep through the secant's slope. That moment is c. It's literally Rolle's theorem applied to the gap between curve and secant: where the gap peaks, curve and secant run parallel.

Same smoothness conditions, any endpoints: ∃ c ∈ (a, b) : f′(c) = f(b) − f(a)b − a

03

Taylor Series

Stand at one point of a smooth function. Match its height, then its slope, then its curvature, then the rate the curvature changes… Each match extends the impersonation further. That's the whole idea of Taylor series.

The intuition

Degree 0 is a flat guess: "the function equals its value here." Degree 1 adds the tangent line — MVT's hero. Degree 2 bends it into the kissing parabola. Every extra term recruits one more derivative at the center and the polynomial hugs the curve over a wider stretch.

Watch the green trust zone below the axis — where the polynomial stays within ±0.05 of the truth. It grows as you add terms. But try ln(1+x): no matter how many terms, the zone slams into a wall at distance 1 from the center — the radius of convergence, where the impersonation breaks down forever.

04

The Hessian Matrix

At a critical point the slope is zero in every direction — so curvature is all that's left. The Hessian collects every second derivative; its eigenvalues tell you whether you're standing in a bowl, on a dome, or on a saddle.

Read the matrix like a face

A = curvature along x, C = curvature along y, B = the twist that couples them. The eigenvalues λ₁, λ₂ are the curvatures along the surface's own principal axes — drawn as arrows on the contour map.

Both λ > 0 → every direction curves up: local minimum. Both λ < 0 → every direction curves down: local maximum. Mixed signs → up one way, down another: saddle — the ball rolls in, then escapes sideways. A zero eigenvalue → flat direction, the test is silent.
